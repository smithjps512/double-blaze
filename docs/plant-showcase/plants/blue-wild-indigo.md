## About

Showy in pollinator gardens.

- Blooms blue in late spring.
- Has a shrubby appearance.
- Host for six butterfly species.
- Attractive black seed pods.

### Fast facts

- Plant type: herbaceous perennial
- Bloom time: late spring to early summer (May to June)
- Mature size: 3 to 4 feet tall and wide
- Hardiness zones: 3 to 9
- Sun: full sun. It tolerates light shade but blooms best in full sun.
- Soil: dry to medium moisture, well draining

### Key features

- Appearance: a shrub-like, bushy mound with bluish green or sage green clover
  like leaves. After blooming it produces large seed pods that turn black and
  rattle in the wind.
- Wildlife value: attracts bees and butterflies, and is a larval host plant for
  butterflies like the wild indigo duskywing.
- Durability: highly drought tolerant and deer resistant once established,
  thanks to a deep taproot.
- History: named "false indigo" because colonial settlers used it to make a blue
  dye that was inferior to true indigo (Indigofera).

### What it is used for

- Natural blue dye for clothing and textiles.
- Pollinator habitat. The blue to purple flower spikes supply nectar and pollen
  for native bees, butterflies and hummingbirds.
- Caterpillar host for the wild indigo duskywing and the hoary edge skipper.
- Soil improvement. It fixes nitrogen because it belongs to the pea family.
- Ornamental planting in perennial borders and cottage gardens.
- Dried seed pods rattle and have been used as toys for children.

## Care

- Flowers best in full sun.
- It can take several years to achieve full height.
- Deep roots make it drought tolerant over time.
- Establishment: it takes 3 to 4 years to reach full size. Potted plants and
  young seedlings dislike being moved because of the long taproot, so the
  permanent location has to be chosen carefully.
- From seed: seeds have a hard coat and need scarification, either rubbing with
  sandpaper or soaking in warm water overnight, before planting.
- Maintenance: virtually none once established, and no dividing. Stems die back
  to the ground in winter.
