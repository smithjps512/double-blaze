# Researching a car page

No em dashes anywhere in this document or any copy it generates.

Your site can now hold a real page per car: what is in it, who built it, and
what happened. Not a paragraph. A page somebody could actually learn from.

There is one rule and everything else follows from it.

## The rule

**You cannot save anything until you have said where you found it.**

Not a suggestion at the bottom of a form. The site will refuse the save and tell
you why, and it refuses on the server, so opening the developer tools and
clicking around it will not work either. That is on purpose, and it is worth
knowing that somebody thought about you specifically when they built it.

**Why it is a rule and not advice.** Anybody can type "the 1970 Charger had 425
horsepower" into a box. The number is either right or it is not, and the only
difference between a page worth reading and a page somebody made up is whether
you can check. Every real reference site works this way. Wikipedia's little
numbers at the end of sentences are exactly this rule.

There is a second reason that matters more to you. In about three weeks somebody
is going to ask you where a number came from, and if you did not write it down
you will have to find it again from nothing. Adding the source takes fifteen
seconds while the tab is still open.

## The order to do it in

The source box is at the **top** of the editor, above everything you write. That
is not a layout accident.

1. **Find a page about your car.** Read it.
2. **Add it as a source**, before you type a word. Title, link, and which part of
   the page it backs up.
3. **Now write.** The tab is open, the facts are in front of you.
4. Found something the first page did not cover? Add that source too, then write
   that bit.

Doing it the other way round means finishing your page, then going back through
your own writing trying to remember which website each sentence came from. That
is genuinely miserable and everybody who does it once never does it twice.

## What goes on a car page

### The technical bit

Short answers. `7.0 litre V8, 425 hp` is better than a paragraph.

| Box | What it means | Where to look |
|---|---|---|
| Manufacturer | Who built it | The obvious one |
| Made | Which years, and roughly how many | Wikipedia's infobox usually has both |
| Engine | Size, layout, power | Enthusiast sites and old road tests |
| Transmission | How many gears, manual or automatic | Often in the same table as the engine |
| Drivetrain | Which wheels the engine drives | Rear wheel drive on most cars this old |
| Chassis and body | What the car is built on and out of | Harder. This is a good one to hunt for |
| Suspension | How the wheels are held on | Often written as "independent front, live rear axle" |
| Brakes | Discs, drums, or one of each | Frequently one of each on a 1960s car, and worth noticing |

**If you cannot find one, leave it empty.** An empty box is honest. A guess is
not, and a guess with a source next to it is worse than a guess on its own,
because now it looks checked.

### The two histories

These are the part that makes it a page rather than a table, and they are
**different research**.

**The story of this car.** Why did it exist? What was it built to beat? What did
people think of it at the time? What happened to it in the end? The best car
stories are usually about a rule somebody was trying to get around.

**The story of who built it.** Who started the company, when, where, and what
else did they make? Most teams do the car and forget this one. It is often the
more interesting half: a lot of car companies started out making something else
entirely.

A few paragraphs each. Leave a blank line between paragraphs and the site will
lay them out for you.

## About the photo

Somebody took that photograph. It is theirs, the same way your drawing is yours.

Putting it on a page anybody can open without saying whose it is is the one
thing on this whole site that could actually get somebody in trouble, so the
site will not let you save a picture without **who it belongs to** and **the
page you got it from**. Both, or nothing saves.

**Where to find one you are allowed to use:**

- **Wikimedia Commons** is the best answer. Every picture tells you the licence
  and the photographer, and you can copy both straight into the boxes.
- **Manufacturer press photos.** Companies put these out wanting them used.
- **A picture off an image search with no page behind it** is the one to avoid.
  If you cannot find who took it, you cannot credit it, so you cannot use it.

"I found it on Google" is not where a photo came from. Google is how you got
there, not the place it lives.

## Copying and pasting

Allowed. Copying a specification table is a sensible thing to do and retyping it
by hand just adds typos.

The editor notices when you paste a big block and asks you two questions:

1. **Is where it came from in your sources?**
2. **Could you explain it out loud?**

The second one is the real test. If somebody asked you what a live rear axle is
and you could not say, then that sentence is not yours yet and it is the exact
sentence you will be asked about. Put it in your own words. Shorter and in your
own words beats longer and borrowed, every single time.

## When is a car page finished

- [ ] At least one source, added before you started writing
- [ ] A photo, with whose it is and where it came from
- [ ] Every technical box you could find an answer for, and none you could not
- [ ] The story of the car
- [ ] The story of the company
- [ ] Every source says which part of the page it backs up
- [ ] You read the whole page back and there is nothing on it you could not
      explain to somebody

Then send the link to somebody who does not care about cars, and see whether
they read to the bottom. That is the actual test.
