A new direction for CTOS

Written with the team, 25 September. A proposal, not a replacement: your own plan and stories are exactly where you left them, and nothing here becomes your plan until you say so.

What we noticed

Your plan lists 17 features and names five apps: Wikipedia, YouTube, TikTok, Snap and Discord. Read quickly, it looks like "all of social media in one app".

Read slowly, it is something else. Messaging, calls, group chats, stickers, reactions, avatars, communities, streaks, user support: almost every feature is about talking to people and being understood by them. The five apps were never the point. They were the clearest examples of how people communicate, and the plan was reaching for that.

So here is the app we think is underneath it.

The idea

A school chat app where everyone writes in their own language and reads in theirs.

A student who has just moved here and is still learning English opens the app in Mandarin or Spanish. A classmate writes "want to sit with us at lunch?" in English and it arrives in Mandarin. The answer goes back in English. Nobody has to wait until their English is good enough to make a friend.

Around the chat sit the things a new student needs in their first weeks: the phrases you need in the hallway, guides to how this school works, a map with room names in your language, and a buddy who has done it before.

Why this is a stronger app

  • You can name the user. "Everyone and anyone" is nobody in particular. A student in their first months at a school where they do not speak the language yet is a real person with a real problem.
  • Every feature has a reason. Streaks are no longer just for fun: they keep a new student talking every day, which is how a language is learned.
  • It is small enough to build. Fourteen features, all about one thing, instead of five apps.
  • Almost none of your work is thrown away. See the table below.

Three things this app has to get right

  1. Translation is sometimes wrong. Every translated message has a Show original button, and a new student can ask "Did I say that right?" without it being embarrassing. An app that hides its mistakes will get somebody laughed at.
  2. The users are children. Only people at the same school can message each other, and anyone can report or block from inside a chat. No strangers, no public profiles.
  3. Which language you speak is personal. It is not on anyone's profile and the app never uses it to sort or search for people. The only place it appears is the small "translated from" note on a message, because the reader needs to know a translation happened.

Questions for the team

  1. Is this your app? If any part of it is not what you meant, change it. This is a draft for you to edit, not a correction.
  2. Should teachers be in it? A student who can message the counsellor in their own language is useful. It also changes who can see what.
  3. Do guests still make sense? For an app at one school, accounts could come from the school instead. Your call.
  4. Is Qiáo the name? The working name is Qiáo (桥), Mandarin for "bridge", with the character 桥 as the logo. It says what the app does, and it is the one decision on the team where a Mandarin speaker is the expert: how it sounds, whether the character looks right as a logo, whether it means what we think it means. If the team wants something else, a quick way to choose: everyone writes three names, and each one has to pass one test, *could a new student who does not speak English yet say it and remember it?* Then vote.
